## Read against the run of 2026-09-06 18:11, and against 1.1.0

Written by hand after the run. This is the SECOND acceptance of 1.2.0: the
first was taken at commit a553685 with 23 of the release's tasks done, and ten
more landed after it, so that report described a build that no longer existed.
Three of the ten touch what these criteria measure — piece completion stopped
using a persistent database (TOR-155), the wall clock gained its own stop
reason (TOR-161), and the top-up's pricing was rewritten (TOR-166).

**The deterministic figures still have not moved.** Criterion 2 ordered 44.0
MiB in 44 pieces, as it has for six releases now, and criterion 1 ordered 104.0
MiB in 104 pieces. Neither the pooled long-lived client, nor dropping the
persistent piece-completion database, nor the new stop reason changed what a
run has to order. Criteria 3 and 4 — the two that lean on what a second run
knows about pieces already on disk, and so the two most exposed to TOR-155 —
both pass with figures within a megabyte of the previous run (12.2 to 12.1 MiB
on the rerun's first pass, 13.1 to 12.1 on criterion 4's second run).

**On webseeds, this run is the evidence the last report said it could not be.**
That report recorded arrivals falling across every criterion after TOR-150
turned webseeds off, and deliberately called it a candidate rather than a
cause, because docs/tor-88-min-traffic-spread.md measured criterion 2's actual
traffic at 45.5–54.6 MiB over 18 runs of the webseed-enabled build (mean 48.8,
sd 3.35) and a single sample cannot be told from that. There are now two
post-webseed samples: **44.5 and 44.7 MiB**. Both sit below the observed
minimum of those 18 runs, and they are 0.2 MiB apart where the old regime
varied over a 9 MiB range. Two draws landing below the minimum of eighteen is
about a 0.3% coincidence if nothing changed, and the tightness is the more
telling half: it is what removing a source of variance looks like. Still two
samples, so this is strong support rather than proof — but it is no longer
indistinguishable from noise.

**The elapsed doubling did NOT replicate, and that matters more.** The last
report noted both elapsed times roughly doubling — 36.1 to 86.7s on criterion
1, 27.5 to 53.0s on criterion 2 — and offered losing the HTTP mirror as the
shape that would explain it. This run came in at **23.5s and 21.8s**, faster
than either earlier figure, on a machine that was substantially MORE loaded
(load 15.88 against 4.99). So the doubling was swarm conditions or machine
noise, not webseeds, and attributing it to the change would have been wrong.
Recording it as a candidate rather than a cause is what kept that mistake out
of the record.

**Which ceiling governed: none.** Both runs completed. No run met its traffic
ceiling, its clock, or the client-wide roof — which is still unset, since
core.DefaultRoof ships unlimited. TOR-161's new distinction between a traffic
stop and a clock stop therefore had nothing to report here; it is exercised by
its own tests, not by this run.

**Criterion 5 keeps the eight measurements it gained.** A private torrent
attached while two public ones fetched in the shared client, DHT off, on its
own port (52242 against the shared client's 52239), and refused outright when
no port was left for a client of its own. The ports differ from the previous
run's because they are OS-assigned; what matters is that they differ from each
other.
